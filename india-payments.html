a payments skill for taking money in india

16 August 2026 · 5 min read · Sri Krishna Ravavarapu · code on GitHub

tl;dr: india-payments is a free, open-source Claude skill for taking money online in India. one-time and recurring, which integration path to pick, the RBI constraints that break subscriptions after launch, and reconciling settlements against your books. it does not tell you which provider to use, on purpose. MIT licensed. get it here.

most payment integrations work on the day they ship. the interesting failures come later.

the disclosure first

i work in payments at Razorpay, which is one of the companies this covers. that is exactly why neither the skill nor this piece recommends a provider. a guide written by someone with a stake, that quietly steers, is worth less than no guide, and readers spot it. so what follows is how the plumbing works and what to ask. the choice stays yours.

the three things people call a gateway

a payment gateway is the technology that moves a transaction to a bank. a payment aggregator onboards you, collects money on your behalf and settles it to you. because it holds customer money in transit, that is a regulated activity: an aggregator needs RBI authorisation and has to keep funds in a designated account with defined settlement timelines. most of what people in India call a gateway is actually an aggregator. a payment orchestrator sits above several providers and routes between them, holding no funds, and is overkill until you have real volume.

the RBI publishes the list of authorised aggregators. checking that whoever will hold your money is on it takes two minutes and decides what happens to your settlements if they fail. almost nobody does it.

how to actually take a payment

five paths, in order of effort. start at the top and only move down when something genuinely rules the simpler one out.

a payment link is a URL you send. no site, no code, minutes. for a freelancer invoicing five clients a month or anyone selling over Instagram this is the right answer, and it gets dismissed because it feels unserious. a payment page is a hosted storefront, still no code. hosted checkout is the default for anyone with a site: their checkout opens over your page, you never touch card data, your PCI scope stays minimal, and you inherit new payment methods without shipping anything. drop-in renders inside your page for more control and more work.

server-to-server means you collect the card details yourself. that puts you in full PCI DSS scope: annual assessment, segmentation, scanning, ongoing cost in money and attention. most people asking for it are chasing a design detail that drop-in already gives them.

whichever path, four things decide whether it survives production. create the order server-side, because anything trusting a client-supplied amount is a way to buy things for one rupee. verify the webhook signature, which a surprising number of live integrations skip, meaning anyone who finds the endpoint can mark orders paid. treat the webhook as the source of truth rather than the browser redirect, because the customer closes the tab and the money still moved. and make the handler idempotent, because webhooks retry and a non-idempotent handler ships the order twice.

recurring, where it gets hard

india has three separate recurring rails, not one. UPI Autopay for consumer subscriptions, registered with a UPI PIN in seconds. e-NACH for bank-account debit, slower to register and suited to larger tickets. card standing instructions under the RBI e-mandate framework. different registration, different limits, different failure modes. offering more than one lifts conversion, because abandonment during registration differs sharply between them.

three rules break integrations, and all three bite after launch rather than during it.

authentication at registration. there is no silent enrolment. any flow assuming a mandate can be created from details you already hold does not work.

a per-transaction ceiling above which authentication is required again. below it, debits run unattended. above it, the customer has to authenticate that specific charge. this is the one that catches people: a subscription working fine at a low price starts failing when you raise the price past the ceiling, and it fails quietly.

a mandatory pre-debit notification a fixed period before each debit. the provider usually sends it, but you have to trigger it with enough lead time. charge the moment a cycle ends and the debit will not present.

then there is tokenisation. you may not store card numbers, so cards on file are network-issued tokens tied to one merchant and one provider. they do not port. switching providers means migrating tokens or asking customers to re-enter cards, and card reissues break tokens on their own, producing a background failure rate that has nothing to do with anyone's balance. ask about exit before you integrate.

design for failure, because failure is routine. retry on a schedule rather than immediately, since a balance problem resolves on salary day. cap the retries. tell the customer what happened with a one-tap fix. and separate involuntary churn from real churn in your metrics, or you will read a plumbing problem as a product problem and go and change the product.

your provider settles net, your books need gross

this is the part nobody plans for. money does not arrive as the customer paid it. it arrives after fees, after GST on those fees, after refunds are netted off, sometimes after a rolling reserve is withheld. chargebacks turn up later and come out of a future payout.

record the settled amount as revenue and you understate turnover, lose the input credit on the fee GST, and end up with books that disagree with your provider's own report in a way that is miserable to unpick a year later. the bank statement shows only the last line, which is why reconciliation cannot be done from the bank alone.

reconcile three ways, monthly: your paid orders, the provider's transaction report, the bank credits. two mismatches matter. an order marked paid with no provider transaction is usually an unverified webhook. a provider transaction with no order means someone paid and never got what they bought, which costs you a customer silently.

how to install india payments

copy the skill folder in:

cp -r india-payments ~/.claude/skills/

on Claude web, upload it as a skill or drop it into a project. then describe the situation: "i sell a course, how do i take payments", "my subscription debits started failing after we raised the price", or "do i need PCI compliance".

questions

which payment provider should i use?

the skill will not tell you, and neither will i. pricing is negotiated, settlement cycles differ, and category acceptance decides more launches than the headline rate does. what it gives you is the questions to ask and what actually varies: effective rate per method at your volume, settlement cycle, rolling reserve, category acceptance, recurring rails, chargeback process, out-of-hours support, and what happens to your card tokens if you leave.

why do subscriptions fail after a price rise?

there is a per-transaction ceiling above which the customer must authenticate each charge. below it, debits run unattended. cross it and every debit needs an action the customer does not know to take. confirm the current threshold before designing around it, and degrade gracefully rather than failing silently.

do i need PCI DSS compliance?

only if you collect card details yourself. hosted checkout and drop-in send card data straight to the provider, keeping your scope at the lightest tier. server-to-server puts you in full scope with an annual assessment and ongoing controls. that cost is the reason to avoid it unless you have volume or a genuine multi-provider routing need.

what's the difference between a gateway, an aggregator and an orchestrator?

a gateway is the technology moving a transaction to a bank. an aggregator onboards you, collects money on your behalf and settles it to you, which is regulated because it holds funds in transit and needs RBI authorisation. an orchestrator routes across several providers and holds nothing. most Indian "gateways" are aggregators.

what are the recurring options in india?

three separate rails. UPI Autopay, registered with a UPI PIN in seconds, for small-ticket consumer subscriptions. e-NACH for bank-account debit, slower to register, suited to larger tickets. card standing instructions under the RBI e-mandate framework. offering more than one lifts conversion, because registration abandonment differs sharply between them.

why don't my settlements match my revenue?

providers settle net. the payout arrives after fees, GST on those fees, refunds netted off and sometimes a rolling reserve, with chargebacks coming out of later payouts. recognise revenue on the transaction date, not the settlement date, and book the fee and its GST separately or you forfeit the input credit.
