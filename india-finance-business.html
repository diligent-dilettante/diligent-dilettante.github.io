a business finance skill that reads your company's books

16 August 2026 · 5 min read · Sri Krishna Ravavarapu · code on GitHub

tl;dr: a free, open-source Claude skill that analyses your company's finances and gives you something to decide on. audited statements, Tally trial balances, Form 3CD, scanned PDFs. it tags what is audited and what is not, computes what growth costs in cash, and leaves out the ratios that need a share price. every figure comes from Python. it is the company-scope sibling of the personal one.

what it does

it analyses an indian private limited company, LLP or proprietorship from the documents the business actually has, rather than from the clean dataset nobody ever has.

point it at a folder of statements and it builds a multi-year series, computes the ratios that apply to an unlisted company, works out what growth costs in cash, and says which of its own numbers you should trust. the output is meant to be decided on, not filed.

why generic financial tools break on indian company accounts

there are several competent financial-analyst skills already. they compute twenty ratios, run a DCF, do budget variance. they share four assumptions that do not hold here.

they assume a jurisdiction that does not exist. one states outright that it applies universal formulas without country-specific adjustment. an indian P&L runs on a statutory classification that a CA, a bank and a registrar all read. restructure it into generic revenue, COGS and opex and anyone official has to rebuild it before they can use it.

the second assumption is clean input. real financials arrive as a scan of a signed statement, a Tally export somebody emailed, and a tax audit report nobody has opened. the ingestion is most of the work.

the third is that every number is equally true. an audited figure and an unaudited trial balance are different kinds of fact.

the last one is the ratio set. a quarter of it is dead on arrival. P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA, PEG all need a market price. a private company has none. computing them anyway produces nulls, which look like missing data rather than an inapplicable question.

which of its own numbers to trust

an audited figure and a number typed into an accounting package are not the same kind of fact, and a company's file usually holds both without saying which is which. every year in the series comes back tagged, and the tag follows the number into the conclusion.

this sounds like bookkeeping until it changes an answer. a partial period read as a full year invents a decline that never happened. a document filed under one name turns out to hold a year everyone thought was missing. both have happened on real books, and both are the kind of error that survives three people looking at the spreadsheet.

why reported profit is not performance

a company mid-capex shows profit falling while cash generation grows. read the profit line alone and you conclude the business is stalling, when it is buying plant. the skill reads it the other way round and says so.

the same correction rescues bad years. a business can lose a third of its revenue and hold cash generation flat, which tells you the cost base is genuinely variable rather than that the company is in trouble. that distinction changes what you do next.

what growth costs in cash

the question owners actually ask is what another rupee of sales costs before any profit arrives. most ratio tools do not answer it.

you get the working capital cycle in days and the cash a rupee of growth ties up, which is the difference between growth that funds itself and growth that quietly needs a bank. a business where the customer pays before the supplier does is structurally safer than one with a better margin, and that rarely shows up in a ratio table.

MSME status is money, not paperwork

for a company under the thresholds, classification carries statutory payment protection and real advantages in public procurement. the skill classifies the company and states what attaches.

it also flags the part nobody models: those advantages are a function of being small and they end as the business grows. a company whose access rests on them is standing on something that gets removed at a threshold, and that belongs in the plan rather than in a surprise.

the group behind the company

family groups fund each other in ways the financial statements do not show. a sister company can be functioning as an undocumented credit line for years without appearing as one anywhere in the accounts.

the skill reads the statutory filings that do disclose it, maps who is funding whom, and sizes the dependency. it is usually unsecured, uncommitted, and larger than anyone assumed. none of it is visible in a ratio.

how to install it

clone the repository and copy the skill folder into your Claude skills directory. 

git clone https://github.com/diligent-dilettante/india-finance.git
cp -r india-finance/india-finance-business ~/.claude/skills/

then ask it something. "read these statements and tell me what they say" is enough to start. it will ask which documents exist before it asks for anything else, because most people do not know what they have until they see the list.

questions

why not just use a generic financial analyst tool?

generic tools assume clean input and a listed company. indian SME financials arrive as scans, Tally exports and tax audit reports, and a quarter of a standard ratio set needs a share price that does not exist. the ingestion and the provenance are most of the work, and generic tools skip both.

what documents does it need?

audited statements for three to five years are the best start, and each carries a comparative column so one document gives two years. a recent trial balance adds currency. Form 3CD adds related-party detail. a Udyam certificate settles classification. it works with whatever subset exists.

does it work on scanned statements?

yes. scans are normal for anything older than about 2018, and for plenty that isn't. every figure it pulls out gets checked against the rest of the statement before it is used, so a misread number fails loudly rather than quietly changing a conclusion.

why are P/E and EV/EBITDA missing?

they need a market price and an unlisted company has none. they are deliberately absent rather than reported as empty. if a valuation is genuinely needed it comes from a DCF or a transaction multiple, with the assumptions stated.

does it do personal finances too?

no. company scope only. india-finance covers one person's own money. separate skills, separate scopes, and neither reads the other's data.

is it free?

yes. MIT licensed, open source, nothing beyond existing Claude access. same repository as india-finance.
